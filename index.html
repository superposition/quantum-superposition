The idea

Strange doesn’t have to mean complicated.

One state.
More than one possibility.

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit can be a combination of both basis states, described by amplitudes. The interesting part is what those amplitudes can do together.

∿∿∿
1. Combine

Possibility has a phase.

Amplitudes have a size and a phase. Add them before you square their magnitude to get a probability. That’s where interference comes from.

There’s room between 0 and 1.

The Bloch sphere maps a pure qubit’s state to a point. The poles are |0⟩ and |1⟩. The equator holds equal-weight superpositions with different phases.

A few useful footnotes

Stay curious.
Keep it quantum.

Is this just a coin we haven’t looked at?+

No. An ordinary unknown coin can be modeled as a mixture of alternatives. A coherent superposition also has a relative phase, which changes interference and measurements in other bases. Try |+⟩ and |−⟩: they have identical Z probabilities but opposite, certain outcomes in X.

Does looking at it make it collapse?+

In this lab, measurement means an ideal projective interaction in a chosen basis. It doesn’t require a conscious observer. After measuring, the state becomes the corresponding basis state. Measuring it immediately again in the same basis gives the same outcome.

What is this simulation simplifying?+

The qubit and measurement calculations use the single-qubit Born rule. The wave field is a stylized two-mode interference illustration with dimensionless coordinates; it is not a full spatial Schrödinger solver or a particle trajectory. We omit noise, decoherence, and interactions with other qubits. Random outcomes come from your browser, not quantum hardware.
